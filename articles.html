Understanding Blood Pressure and How It Is Measured

Nursing & Health Education • G Nurse Research Hub Nigeria

Blood pressure is one of the measurements commonly taken when assessing a person's health. It describes the pressure exerted by circulating blood against the walls of the arteries.

Blood pressure is usually recorded using two numbers. The first is systolic pressure and the second is diastolic pressure.

What Is Systolic Pressure?

Systolic pressure refers to the pressure in the arteries when the heart contracts and pumps blood into the circulation.

What Is Diastolic Pressure?

Diastolic pressure refers to the pressure in the arteries while the heart relaxes between contractions.

Preparing for a Blood Pressure Measurement

Several factors can affect a blood pressure reading. For an accurate measurement, the person should be positioned appropriately and given time to rest before the measurement when circumstances permit.

  • The person should be comfortably seated where appropriate.
  • The arm should be supported.
  • The cuff should be appropriate for the person's arm size.
  • The measurement should be taken using an appropriate technique.
  • The reading should be recorded accurately.

Why Technique Matters

Incorrect cuff placement, poor positioning, movement and other factors can influence a blood pressure reading. Nurses and other healthcare professionals therefore need to follow appropriate measurement procedures.

Important: A single blood pressure reading does not necessarily provide a complete picture of a person's health. Persistent abnormal readings should be discussed with an appropriate healthcare professional.

Key Learning Point

Accurate blood pressure measurement is a basic but important clinical skill. Correct preparation, equipment selection, positioning, measurement and documentation all contribute to reliable assessment.

Hand Hygiene in Nursing Practice

Nursing Practice • Infection Prevention Education

Hand hygiene is an important part of infection prevention in healthcare environments. Hands can come into contact with patients, equipment, surfaces and body fluids during routine care.

Appropriate hand hygiene helps reduce the transfer of microorganisms between people and the healthcare environment.

When Should Hand Hygiene Be Considered?

Healthcare workers should perform hand hygiene at appropriate points during patient care and whenever their hands may have become contaminated.

  • Before appropriate patient contact.
  • Before clean or aseptic procedures.
  • After exposure to body fluids or contamination risks.
  • After patient contact.
  • After contact with the patient's surroundings when indicated.

Handwashing

When handwashing is required, hands should be cleaned thoroughly using appropriate materials and technique. Attention should be given to areas that are commonly missed.

Commonly Missed Areas

  • Between the fingers.
  • Thumbs.
  • Fingertips.
  • Backs of the hands.
  • Areas around the nails.
Learning tip: Nursing students should learn the recommended hand hygiene technique from their institution and current infection-prevention guidance.

Why It Matters

Hand hygiene is a simple practice, but consistent application is an important part of safe healthcare delivery and infection prevention.

Understanding Vital Signs in Nursing

Nursing Fundamentals • Clinical Assessment

Vital signs are basic physiological measurements used during patient assessment. They can provide useful information about a person's current condition and changes over time.

Common Vital Signs

  • Temperature.
  • Pulse.
  • Respiratory rate.
  • Blood pressure.
  • Oxygen saturation when clinically indicated.

Temperature

Body temperature can be measured using an appropriate thermometer and method. The method used should be documented where relevant because readings can vary according to the measurement site and technique.

Pulse

Pulse assessment may include rate, rhythm and strength. Changes from a person's usual condition may require further assessment depending on the clinical situation.

Respiratory Rate

Respiratory assessment involves observing breathing and counting respirations appropriately. The nurse may also consider the pattern and effort of breathing.

Blood Pressure

Blood pressure provides information about pressure within the arterial circulation and is commonly measured using an appropriate cuff and device.

Oxygen Saturation

A pulse oximeter can be used to estimate peripheral oxygen saturation. The result should be interpreted together with the patient's clinical condition and the limitations of the device.

Important: Vital-sign values should be interpreted in context. Age, activity, medications, illness and other factors may affect measurements.

Documentation

Accurate recording of vital signs allows healthcare workers to compare current observations with previous measurements and recognize changes that may require further assessment.

Patient Safety in Nursing Care

Nursing Practice • Patient Safety Education

Patient safety involves reducing avoidable risks and supporting safe care throughout a patient's interaction with healthcare services.

Identify the Patient Correctly

Appropriate patient identification is important before medications, procedures, specimen collection and other clinical activities.

Communicate Clearly

Clear communication between healthcare professionals helps support continuity of care. Important information should be communicated and documented according to local procedures.

Medication Safety

Nurses should follow approved medication administration procedures and verify relevant information before administering medicines.

Where applicable, this may include checking the medication, patient, dose, route, timing and other required information according to institutional policy.

Prevent Falls

Patients who have difficulty walking, weakness, dizziness or other risk factors may require additional safety measures.

Observe Changes

Nurses frequently observe patients for changes in condition. Unexpected changes should be assessed and communicated through the appropriate clinical process.

Key point: Patient safety is not one single action. It involves communication, assessment, identification, documentation, infection prevention and adherence to appropriate clinical procedures.

Nursing Documentation: Why Accurate Records Matter

Nursing Practice • Documentation and Communication

Nursing documentation is an important component of healthcare communication. Patient records can contain information about assessments, care provided, observations, interventions and responses to care.

Why Documentation Matters

Accurate records can help members of the healthcare team understand what has happened during a patient's care and support continuity between different shifts and healthcare professionals.

Characteristics of Good Documentation

  • Accurate.
  • Clear.
  • Relevant.
  • Timely.
  • Objective.
  • Consistent with applicable professional and institutional requirements.

Objective Documentation

Nurses should distinguish observed facts from assumptions or unsupported conclusions. Where appropriate, observations should be documented clearly and professionally.

Timing

Documentation should be completed according to applicable clinical policies and professional requirements. Delayed or incomplete records can make it more difficult to understand the sequence of care.

Confidentiality

Patient information is sensitive. Healthcare workers should handle records according to applicable privacy, confidentiality and professional requirements.

Learning point: Nursing documentation is not simply paperwork. It is part of communication, continuity of care and professional practice.

Preventing Dehydration: Basic Health Education

Health Education • Fluid Balance

Dehydration occurs when the body loses more fluid than it takes in. Fluid requirements vary between individuals and can also change with illness, activity, environmental conditions and other circumstances.

Possible Causes

  • Inadequate fluid intake.
  • Vomiting.
  • Diarrhea.
  • Heavy sweating.
  • Fever and increased fluid losses.
  • Some illnesses or medical conditions.

Possible Signs

Depending on the severity and cause, dehydration may be associated with thirst, dry mouth, reduced urination, weakness or dizziness.

More serious dehydration can cause significant clinical problems and requires appropriate medical assessment.

Basic Prevention

  • Drink appropriate amounts of fluids regularly.
  • Increase attention to fluid needs during hot conditions or activity.
  • Pay attention to fluid losses caused by illness.
  • Seek appropriate healthcare advice when symptoms are concerning.

When to Seek Medical Help

A person with severe weakness, confusion, inability to keep fluids down, very low urine output, significant deterioration or other concerning symptoms should receive appropriate medical assessment.

Important: This article provides general health education and does not diagnose dehydration or determine an individual's fluid requirements.
